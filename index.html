Understanding satellite latitude coverage

A satellite's orbit determines where it travels over Earth, while its viewing geometry determines which nearby locations it can access. This guide shows how those two factors combine to determine coverage along a chosen latitude during one orbit.

Step 1 · The path

Orbital inclination determines the ground-track limits

Maximum ground-track latitude ≈ orbital inclination i

The ground track is the path traced on Earth's surface by the point directly beneath the satellite (the subsatellite point). Because the orbital plane is inclined, this path moves north and south as the satellite travels.

For a circular prograde orbit with inclination i = 55°, the ground track reaches approximately 55° N and 55° S. These are the turning latitudes; the satellite itself remains in space above the surface.

Notice that the ground track does not need to pass directly over a location for the satellite to access it.

Step 2 · The sensor

The access corridor extends beyond the ground track

Northernmost accessible latitude ≈ i + λ

Imagine a strip of Earth's surface extending to either side of the ground track. This is the satellite's access corridor: the region it could potentially observe under the chosen viewing constraints.

The access half-angle λ is the angle at Earth's centre between the subsatellite point and the edge of the accessible region. It depends on altitude and viewing restrictions, such as a minimum elevation angle.

For example, if i = 55° and λ = 20°, the corridor can extend northward to approximately 75° N. This is a geometric access limit, not necessarily the actual swath of an imaging instrument.

Step 3 · Choose a latitude

Coverage is measured along a latitude circle

The calculator measures the total length of accessible longitude intervals ÷ the full 360° latitude circle.

Now choose a target latitude, such as 20° N. Imagine the circle formed by all points around Earth at that latitude. Every point on this circle has the same latitude but a different longitude.

As the satellite completes an orbit, its access corridor intersects certain portions of this latitude circle. Other portions remain outside the corridor.

The calculator finds the fraction of longitudes along this latitude circle that fall within the idealized access corridor during one orbit. It is not calculating the fraction of Earth's total surface area.

Step 4 · The three cases

Why are there zero, one, or two coverage regions?

Zero regions: The target latitude lies beyond the northern reach of the access corridor, so none of its longitudes can be accessed.

One region: Near the orbit's northernmost point, the accessible longitude intervals associated with the northbound and southbound passes merge into one continuous region.

Two regions: At lower northern latitudes, those intervals are separated, leaving two distinct accessible longitude regions.

For i = 55° and λ = 20°, compare the results at 80° N, 50° N, and 20° N. These illustrate the three cases.

Step 5 · Interpret the number

What does a coverage percentage represent?

Coverage (%) = (accessible longitude width / 360°) × 100

Suppose the result is 49.3% at 50° N. This means that, in the idealized single-orbit geometry, the access corridor covers approximately 49.3% of the possible longitude positions along the 50° N latitude circle.

It does not mean that the satellite sees 49.3% of Earth at one instant. It also does not directly give imaging swath, continuous visibility, or revisit time.

Try changing the inclination, access half-angle, and target latitude in the calculator. Observe when the accessible longitude intervals widen, merge, separate, or disappear.

Learn the geometry

Why does latitude change satellite coverage?

An inclined orbit crosses the equator twice and reaches a maximum north/south latitude equal to its inclination (for a prograde orbit). A sensor may also observe targets on either side of the ground track. The combination creates a corridor of potential access on Earth.

Choose one latitude and imagine tracing a full circle around Earth at that latitude. The calculator estimates what fraction of that circle intersects the satellite’s access corridor over one idealized orbit.

Three possible cases

0 regions: The target latitude lies beyond the farthest reachable latitude: Lat > i + λ.

1 region: The ascending and descending coverage corridors join near the orbit’s northernmost extent: i − λ < Lat < i + λ.

2 regions: At lower northern latitudes, the ascending and descending corridors intersect the latitude circle separately: 0 ≤ Lat < i − λ.

At exact case boundaries, intervals may touch or shrink to zero. The schematic is intended to explain the cases, not reproduce exact projected geography.

How to read 49.3% coverage

It means that approximately 49.3% of the longitudes along the 50° N latitude circle fall within the idealized access corridor at some time during one orbit. It does not mean the satellite sees 49.3% of Earth's surface simultaneously, or that a particular ground station has a guaranteed access pass on 49.3% of successive orbits.

What is the access half-angle?

λ is an Earth-centered angle measured from the subsatellite ground track to the outer access boundary. It is not the sensor's spacecraft-centered off-nadir angle. A minimum elevation angle, altitude, and sensor pointing limits determine how far the instrument can actually observe. The calculator assumes access is possible at all off-track angles from 0 to λ on either side.

Model assumptions and limitations

The formulas use idealized circular-orbit geometry, northern latitudes, a prograde inclination below 90°, and a symmetric access corridor. They omit Earth rotation during the orbit, J₂ perturbations, terrain, sensor imaging width and pointing restrictions, and actual revisit-time simulation. Use numerical orbit propagation for operational mission design.
